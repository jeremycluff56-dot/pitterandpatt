Letters to Baby: How a Baby Shower Time Capsule Works

Hosting help from Pitter & Pat

Games make a shower fun. A letters-to-baby time capsule makes it unforgettable. The idea is simple: at the shower, each guest writes a letter to the baby — then seals it in an envelope marked with a future milestone. Baby (with mom and dad) opens one letter at a time: on the first birthday, the first day of school, the sixteenth birthday, all the way to eighteen.

Imagine being sixteen and reading what your grandmother hoped for you before you were born. That's the gift this activity creates — and it takes about fifteen minutes of the party.

How it works, step by step

  • Set up a letter station. A small table with the letter cards, envelopes, pens, and a sign explaining the idea. Guests drift over whenever there's a quiet moment — no big group announcement needed.
  • Each guest picks a prompt. Prompts solve the "I don't know what to write" problem: a letter for the 1st birthday, one for the first day of school, one of advice, one of predictions, one for the 18th birthday.
  • They write, seal, and label. Each envelope gets a milestone seal — "Open on your 5th birthday" — so the right letter waits for the right day.
  • One letter is read aloud. A single "open today" letter, written for the shower itself, gets read to the room. Everything else stays sealed — that's what makes the future openings special.
  • The letters go home in a keepsake box. Labeled, logged, and stored somewhere safe, ready for eighteen years of birthdays.

Tips for a station that runs itself

Put the station near drinks or dessert, where guests naturally linger. Set out more pens than cards. If your crowd includes shy writers, the prompt cards do the heavy lifting — a good prompt ("Tell baby about the day they were born into this family…") turns a blank page into three paragraphs. And take one photo of the pile of sealed envelopes; it's the picture mom will share.

What you need

Letter cards with prompts, envelopes, milestone seals or labels, a sign for the station, a log so the family can track which letters exist, and a box to keep them in. You can assemble it from craft-store pieces — or use a printable kit that has every piece designed to match.
